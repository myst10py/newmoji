# Media Literacy Curriculum

## Overview
NewMoji's education system is embedded in consumption, not taught separately. Every story is an opportunity to build critical thinking skills. This document outlines the core curriculum and how it's delivered.

---

## Core Learning Objectives

By completing the NewMoji curriculum, users will be able to:

1. **Identify bias** in headlines and articles
2. **Evaluate source credibility** using clear criteria
3. **Distinguish fact from opinion** in news coverage
4. **Recognize loaded language** and emotional manipulation
5. **Cross-reference claims** across multiple sources
6. **Understand framing** and perspective differences
7. **Spot common misinformation techniques**
8. **Practice healthy news consumption** habits

---

## Learning Paths

### Path 1: News Consumer Basics (Beginner)
**Duration:** 7 days, ~5-10 min/day
**Unlocks:** Immediately available

**Day 1: Beyond the Headline**
```
💡 Teachable Moment:
"Most people only read headlines. But headlines are
designed to grab attention, not tell the whole story."

🎯 Challenge:
Read 3 stories all the way to Level 2 today.
Notice what the headline left out.

🏆 Achievement: "Deep Reader"
```

**Day 2: What is a Source?**
```
💡 Lesson:
Every news story comes from somewhere. A "source"
can be:
• News organizations (CNN, AP, Reuters)
• Individual journalists
• Primary sources (documents, eyewitnesses)
• Secondary sources (reports, analyses)

🎯 Challenge:
Identify the original source in today's stories.
Was it a press release? A research paper? An interview?

🏆 Achievement: "Source Spotter"
```

**Day 3: Fact vs Opinion**
```
💡 Lesson:
Fact: "The temperature was 95°F"
Opinion: "The weather was unbearably hot"

Even in news, opinions sneak in through word choice.

🎯 Challenge:
Read these 5 sentences. Mark which are facts and
which include opinion.

Example:
"Congress passed a controversial spending bill" ← Opinion!
("controversial" is a judgment)

"Congress passed a $1.2T spending bill" ← Fact!
(verifiable numbers)

🏆 Achievement: "Fact Checker"
```

**Day 4: Loaded Language**
```
💡 Lesson:
Loaded language = words that trigger emotions

Examples:
• Neutral: "protest"
• Loaded (positive): "demonstration"
• Loaded (negative): "mob"

Same event, different feelings triggered.

🎯 Challenge:
Find the loaded language in these headlines:

"Hero whistleblower exposes corruption"
vs
"Disgruntled employee leaks documents"

Same person, different framing!

🏆 Achievement: "Word Detective"
```

**Day 5: Left, Right, Center**
```
💡 Lesson:
Most news sources have a general perspective:

Left-leaning: Emphasize social issues, government
programs, environmental protection

Right-leaning: Emphasize limited government, free
markets, traditional values

Center: Try to present both sides, focus on process

None are inherently "fake" - they just have different
values and audiences.

🎯 Challenge:
Look at today's story perspectives. Can you identify
which sources are left/center/right based on their
headline emphasis?

🏆 Achievement: "Perspective Pro"
```

**Day 6: Why Bias Exists**
```
💡 Lesson:
Bias isn't always bad or intentional. It exists because:

• Journalists are human with perspectives
• News organizations have specific audiences
• Stories require choosing what to include/exclude
• Word choice always carries some connotation

The goal isn't to find "unbiased" news (impossible).
It's to recognize bias and read multiple perspectives.

🎯 Challenge:
Read the same story from left, center, and right sources.
What does each one emphasize? What do they leave out?

🏆 Achievement: "Multi-Perspective Master"
```

**Day 7: Your Critical Thinking Foundation**
```
🎉 Path Complete!

You've learned to:
✓ Read beyond headlines
✓ Identify sources
✓ Spot fact vs opinion
✓ Recognize loaded language
✓ Understand perspectives
✓ See why bias exists

Your media literacy score: 🎓

You're now better equipped to consume news than
most adults. No joke.

[Share your achievement]
[Choose next learning path]
```

### Path 2: Source Credibility (Intermediate)
**Duration:** 7 days
**Prerequisite:** Complete News Consumer Basics

**Day 1: What Makes a Source Credible?**
```
💡 Lesson:
Credible sources:
✓ Cite their sources
✓ Correct mistakes transparently
✓ Separate news from opinion clearly
✓ Have editorial standards
✓ Are transparent about funding
✓ Have a track record

This doesn't mean they're always right or unbiased!
It means they follow journalistic standards.

🎯 Challenge:
Evaluate 3 news sources using this checklist.
```

**Day 2: Red Flags for Fake News**
```
💡 Lesson:
Warning signs of unreliable sources:
🚩 ALL CAPS HEADLINES!!!
🚩 No author listed
🚩 Extreme emotional language
🚩 "They don't want you to know this!"
🚩 No other sources reporting it
🚩 Claims "everyone is saying"
🚩 Website designed to look like real news

🎯 Challenge:
We'll show you 5 headlines. Identify the fake ones.
```

**Day 3: Understanding News Wire Services**
```
💡 Lesson:
AP, Reuters, Bloomberg = "wire services"
They report facts that other news organizations
then add analysis to.

When you see "AP reports..." in multiple places,
that's the original source everyone is citing.

Wire services are generally more neutral because
they serve everyone.

🎯 Challenge:
Find the wire service article today's story came from.
See how different outlets added their own spin.
```

**Day 4: Primary vs Secondary Sources**
```
💡 Lesson:
Primary = Original document, data, or eyewitness
Secondary = Someone reporting on the primary source

Always better to check the primary source when possible!

Example:
Primary: The actual research paper
Secondary: News article about the paper

🎯 Challenge:
Today's story mentions a study. Find the actual study
(we'll link it). What did the news coverage get right?
What nuance did they miss?
```

**Day 5: Fact-Checking Organizations**
```
💡 Lesson:
Organizations that specialize in fact-checking:
• Snopes.com
• FactCheck.org
• PolitiFact
• Full Fact (UK)
• Africa Check

They verify claims made by politicians, media, and
social media posts.

🎯 Challenge:
A politician made a claim in today's news. Check if
fact-checkers have verified it. What did you find?
```

**Day 6: Social Media vs News**
```
💡 Lesson:
Social media posts ≠ news

Even when they're from journalists or officials:
• May lack context
• May be quickly posted without verification
• May be misinterpreted
• May be deliberately misleading

Always verify before sharing.

🎯 Challenge:
We'll show you 3 viral social media posts about today's
news. Which ones are accurate? Which are misleading?
```

**Day 7: Your Credibility Toolkit**
```
🎉 Path Complete!

You can now:
✓ Evaluate source credibility
✓ Spot fake news red flags
✓ Understand wire services
✓ Find primary sources
✓ Use fact-checking tools
✓ Verify social media claims

You're now in the top 10% of news consumers. Seriously.

[Share achievement]
[Choose next path]
```

### Path 3: Understanding Bias (Intermediate)
**Duration:** 10 days
**Prerequisite:** Complete News Consumer Basics

**Modules include:**
- Selection bias (what stories get covered)
- Framing bias (how stories are presented)
- Omission bias (what's left out)
- Headline bias (summarizing with spin)
- Visual bias (photos and graphics)
- Confirmation bias (your own biases)

---

### Path 4: Advanced Critical Thinking (Advanced)
**Duration:** 14 days
**Prerequisite:** Complete 2 previous paths

**Modules include:**
- Propaganda techniques
- Statistical manipulation
- False equivalence
- Strawman arguments
- Appeal to emotion vs logic
- Detecting coordinated disinformation
- Media ownership and incentives

## Teachable Moments (Embedded in Stories)

### Micro-Lessons (appear in Level 2)
Short, single-concept callouts that take 10-15 seconds to read.

**Examples:**

```
💡 Notice how this quote is from a "spokesperson" not
   named. This is common but makes it harder to verify.
```

```
💡 The word "allegedly" means it's not proven yet.
   News must use this for legal reasons.
```

```
💡 See how this story cites "a study" without naming it?
   That's a red flag. Always look for specific sources.
```

```
💡 This headline uses a question: "Is X bad for you?"
   Questions in headlines often mean "we don't know but
   want clicks."
```

### Challenges (appear in Level 3)
Interactive exercises that test understanding.

**Types:**

**1. Spot the Bias**
```
Which of these headlines is most neutral?

[ ] "Republicans block crucial legislation"
[ ] "Senate vote fails along party lines"
[ ] "Democrats fail to win GOP support"

Answer: B (describes process without taking sides)
```

**2. Source Credibility Quiz**
```
Would you trust this source? Why or why not?

Source: "TotallyRealNews.com"
- No author listed
- All caps headline
- Extreme claims
- No other sources reporting it

[ ] Trust it
[ ] Don't trust it

Why? [List the red flags]
```

**3. Fact vs Opinion**
```
Mark each statement:

□ Fact  □ Opinion: "The bill passed 214-209"
□ Fact  □ Opinion: "The controversial bill passed narrowly"
□ Fact  □ Opinion: "Congress made a terrible mistake"

Answers: Fact, Opinion (controversial=judgment), Opinion
```

**4. Compare Perspectives**
```
We'll show you 3 headlines about the same event.
What does each one emphasize? What do they omit?

Headline 1: [Left source]
Headline 2: [Center source]
Headline 3: [Right source]

[Open-ended response]
```

**5. Trace the Source**
```
This article says "experts say X."

Follow the citations to find:
- Who are these experts?
- Where did they say this?
- What's the primary source?

[Multiple choice or open-ended]
```

## Adaptive Learning

### Difficulty Adjustment
- If user consistently gets challenges right → harder challenges
- If user struggles → simpler explanations, more scaffolding
- If user skips challenges → make them more engaging/relevant

### Personalization
- Learn which topics they engage with most
- Suggest learning paths based on interests
- Adjust reading level if needed

### Mastery-Based Progression
- Can't skip to advanced paths without completing prerequisites
- Must demonstrate understanding, not just click through
- Retake assessments if score too low

---

## Content Delivery Principles

### 1. Show, Don't Tell
Instead of: "Headlines can be biased"
Show: 3 different headlines, ask them to spot differences

### 2. Embedded, Not Separate
Learning happens while consuming news, not in a separate "education" section

### 3. Optional But Rewarded
Never force educational content, but make it rewarding (points, badges, score improvement)

### 4. Bite-Sized
No lesson longer than 5 minutes
Most teachable moments: 10-30 seconds

### 5. Immediately Applicable
Learn something → use it in the next story
Theory → Practice loop

### 6. Celebrate Progress
Constant positive reinforcement
"You're getting better!" not "You got it wrong"

### 7. Respectful Tone
Never condescending
Acknowledge this is hard for everyone
Many adults don't have these skills
